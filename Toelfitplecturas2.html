The Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

Put in the simplest terms, the GDP, or gross domestic product, of a country is the value in money terms of the total amount of final goods and services produced by its economy during a particular period of time. This measure is important for its own sake and because it helps economists and others understand both inflation and unemployment. Governments watch the GDP figures avidly because these figures show how prosperous a country is and because they are useful in forecasting the future health of the economy. All in all, it is no exaggeration to say that the gross domestic product is one of the most closely watched numbers in existence.

Although the measurement of the GDP may seem straightforward, this is by no means the case. First, gross domestic product does not include the values of all the goods and services produced; it includes only the final goods and services produced, those destined for the ultimate user. For example, wheat flour purchased for family consumption is a final good, but flour to be used in manufacturing bread commercially is an intermediate good, not a final good. To avoid double counting, the output of intermediate goods must not be included in the GDP.

Second, some final goods and services that must be counted in the gross domestic product are not bought and sold in the marketplace, so they are valued at what they cost. Consider the services performed by the civil authorities: police protection, fire protection, the court system, defense, and so forth. Such services are not bought or sold, yet they are an important part of an economy’s final output. Economists and statisticians have decided to value them at what they cost the taxpayers. This is not ideal, but it is the best practical solution advanced to date.

Third, for practical reasons it is necessary to omit certain types of final output from the gross domestic product, in particular the services of those who work for their families at home. This is not because economists fail to appreciate these services but because it is extremely difficult to get reasonably reliable estimates of their money value.

Historic Preservation and Cultural Patrimony

In the United States, some people wish to preserve old buildings for historical value. Others dislike a newly built environment. During the 1960s, Christopher Tunnard, one of the pioneers of the preservation movement, proposed a more inclusive rationale for preserving the past. He believed it was society's responsibility to cultivate what he called the "cultural patrimony."

Cultural patrimony means inheritance from one's ancestors. It includes places of historical significance, artistic prominence, social import, or aesthetic value. Thus, society's responsibility is not simply to preserve what we receive from our ancestors and pass it on to future generations. It is to select what is significant, nurture and enhance it, make improvements, and pass on to the next generation an environment that is richer, more fulfilling, and more beautiful.

Preserving old buildings or historic places can provide tremendous benefits to a community. Besides the economic benefits from the tourists it attracts, social benefits from a more heterogeneous population, and cultural benefits from its enhanced setting for artistic activity, these benefits often offset the costs of preservation. A good example is the complete 18th-century village of Williamsburg, Virginia.

However, there are not enough funds to create a mini-Williamsburg everywhere, nor are there enough tourists to visit them or curators to maintain them. Moreover, there is every reason to keep from transforming the North American landscape into one continuous museum. Instead we should find ways to make the cultural patrimony an integral part of daily life. That means retaining landmarks without destroying their continuing utility.

Evolution of Avian Egg Coloration

All of the earliest birds' eggs were probably white, but egg color most likely evolved as a way to camouflage (disguise) eggs from predators. There are two facts that support this theory. First, most birds that still lay white eggs build their nests in cavities, burrows, or covered nests. Second, those birds that lay white eggs in open nests either sit on them in order to begin the process immediately after the first egg is laid or cover their eggs to hide them when they must leave the nest. Most ground-nesting species blend their eggs into their surroundings, which means that a speckled or brownish color if the bird is laying its eggs on dead vegetation, a light sandy color if it nests on beaches or deserts, or even black if it builds its nest on ground that has been burned.

The family of thrushes is one whose members eggs vary according to nesting site. Ground-nesting thrushes produce brown, gray, or olive-colored eggs. Those that build their nests in the forest produce heavily speckled eggs, while those that nest in the crevices between rocks have speckled, greenish, or plain blue eggs. The thrushes that lay their eggs in holes produce white eggs. Ornithologists (scientists who study birds) have experimented with egg color to determine its common role. One key factor in protecting the developing chick within from its enemies.
